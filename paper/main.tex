\begin{abstract}
Decision models return option probabilities for typed questions in one forward pass, and applications act on the top answer. We test TypeSafe's closed Jev and five open decision models with the gender-bias studies of \citet{fulgu-capraro-2024-surprising}, BBQ, StereoSet and a gender-swapped decision task \citep{tamkin-etal-2023-discrimination}. All six rate violence for a greater good as more acceptable against a man than against a woman, in every act and stake. Asked who wrote a child's phrase without a gender cue, two open models answer ``girl'' in 82--85\% of cases, but most abstain when they may. In ambiguous BBQ gender questions Jev always abstains; the open models commit in 8--61\%, mostly to the stereotyped one. In the decision task, five of the six give women significantly more favourable answers (+0.05 to +0.10 in log-odds); a gender swap changes 1--3.5\% of top answers (Jev's partly through call-to-call variation). The top answer turns leans into firm decisions (clef-flash: 0.59 to 0.89), and a confidence gate removes many but mostly not their direction. Two open models described as students of Jev are 3--9 times further from it on our questions than their cards report for their own test set.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Decision models are built to act. In September 2026 TypeSafe AI released Jev, ``a new class of frontier models built to make fast, structured decisions that software can use directly'' \citep{typesafe-2026-jev}: it answers typed questions (pick one of these options, score this on a scale, yes or no) with option probabilities from one forward pass, through a closed API. Open decision models from Perplexity, Cloudflare, IBM, Liquid AI and Jared Palmer can be served through llama.cpp's \texttt{/v1/systemone} endpoint \citep{perplexity-2026-pplx,cloudflare-2026-clef-flash,ibm-2026-granite-switch,liquidai-2026-d1,palmer-2026-kev}; the clef-flash and Kev cards state compatibility with Jev's API. AutoTrust's cards describe its open JEV models as students of Jev 1.13 \citep{autotrust-2026-jev9b,autotrust-2026-jev27b}. An application acts on the top answer, so a bias can become a different decision about a person: an allocational harm, not only a representational one \citep{gallegos-etal-2024-bias}.

The makers add caveats. AutoTrust writes ``Not for high-stakes decisions'' \citep{autotrust-2026-jev9b}; Kev's model card says ``Do not use it for consequential automated decisions about people without human review. Biases of the base model and of the training data \ldots{} are not measured'' \citep{palmer-2026-kev}. We found no bias measurement in the cards and announcements we checked.

\citet{fulgu-capraro-2024-surprising} found that GPT-4 attributed masculine-stereotyped children's phrases to girls far more readily than feminine ones to boys, and judged violence against a man more acceptable than against a woman when a nuclear apocalypse was at stake. We ask whether such biases appear in decision models. Our contributions:

\begin{enumerate}
\item A bias evaluation of Jev and five open decision models at their released 16-bit precision (the 27B model in 8-bit): the original paradigms extended by prompt variants, five stakes and an abstention option; BBQ in nine categories; StereoSet; and a gender-swapped decision task that tests the allocation claim directly.
\item A comparison of probability-level measures with the argmax (top-answer) decision, with and without the makers' confidence gate.
\item A comparison of two open models described as students of Jev with their teacher.
\item Controls for base model, interface, numeric precision and serving path.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Gender asymmetries in moral judgements.} \citet{fulgu-capraro-2024-surprising} reported seven studies on GPT models; we follow the numbering of this arXiv version (journal version: \citealp{fulgu-capraro-2024-chbr}). Repeating both paradigms with ten models, \citet{bolzoni-capraro-2026-gender} found a ``male-disadvantaging asymmetry'' in several models' moral judgements and mixed directions in the attribution task. Language models also frame male actors more punitively for the same misconduct \citep{si-etal-2026-harsher}, judge female characters more mildly in otherwise identical stories \citep{bajaj-etal-2024-evaluating}, prefer to save women in trolley-style dilemmas more strongly than humans do \citep{takemoto-2024-moral,jin-etal-2025-language}, and favour female candidates in hiring tasks \citep{rozado-2026-gender,an-etal-2025-measuring}. None of these studies tests decision models.

\paragraph{Benchmarks.} \citet{blodgett-etal-2021-stereotyping} question what benchmarks such as BBQ \citep{parrish-etal-2022-bbq} and StereoSet \citep{nadeem-etal-2021-stereoset} measure, and \citet{lum-etal-2025-bias} find no significant correlation between standard bias metrics and metrics of bias in more realistic use. BBQ results change with the prompt \citep{hida-etal-2025-social,mizrahi-etal-2024-state}, and its ambiguous-context score can reward indiscriminate abstention, the refusal to answer \citep{manduru-domeniconi-2026-beyond,wen-etal-2025-know}. Forcing a choice changes what models answer \citep{rottger-etal-2024-political}, which Studies 1 and 1u compare directly.

\paragraph{Decisions and readouts.} \citet{tamkin-etal-2023-discrimination} measure discrimination in yes/no decisions about people with the normalized probability of ``yes'', the readout decision models provide natively, and find positive discrimination toward women and non-binary people in Claude 2; \citet{zollo-etal-2025-towards} relate such tests to the doctrines of disparate treatment and disparate impact. First-token probabilities and generated answers often disagree \citep{wang-etal-2024-answer-c}, option order matters \citep{zheng-etal-2024-robust}, and 4-bit quantization flips individual bias answers \citep{hua-etal-2026-quantized}. Since ``bias amplification'' denotes a model exaggerating a correlation in its training data \citep{zhao-etal-2017-men}, we speak of the argmax decision instead.

\section{Bias statement}
\label{sec:bias}

Following \citet{blodgett-etal-2020-language}, we state which system behaviours we count as bias, why they are harmful, and to whom, distinguishing allocational harms (a disparate distribution of resources or opportunities: direct or indirect discrimination) from representational harms such as stereotyping \citep{gallegos-etal-2024-bias}.

\paragraph{What we count as bias.} (i) \emph{Counterfactual dependence}: an output that changes when only the stated gender of a person changes (Study 2, the vignette of Study 2d, the decision task). (ii) \emph{Stereotype filling}: a committed answer that follows a stereotype where the input holds no information on gender, or on which person fits a role (uncued phrases in Study 1, ambiguous BBQ questions). (iii) \emph{Stereotype preference}: more probability on a stereotyped than on an anti-stereotyped completion (StereoSet). Following evidence in the input is accuracy, and abstaining when information is missing is the correct answer.

\paragraph{Why harmful, and to whom.} In the decision task, a lower probability of the favourable answer for one gender would be disparate treatment if an application acted on it: an allocational harm. No test involves a deployed application, and the other tests measure answers that are not allocations, so we treat them as representational harms. Stereotyping falls on the stereotyped group (women, men and trans people in BBQ's gender questions, and the groups of eight more categories); labelling uncued writing a girl's misrepresents boys and children outside the binary.

\paragraph{Ratings that disfavour male victims.} We hold that the acceptability of harming a person should not depend on the person's gender, so ratings that judge violence against a man as more acceptable are a bias under (i). This is a normative position, not an empirical finding. Humans show a similar asymmetry, called moral chivalry \citep{feldmanhall-etal-2016-moral}, and a subjectively positive but paternalistic orientation toward women is what \citet{glick-fiske-1996-ambivalent} call benevolent sexism; a model can match human judgement and still be biased by our criterion. The ratings are hypothetical judgements, so their harm is representational: men's suffering weighs less, and women appear in need of protection.

\paragraph{Gender terms.} We use each source's categories (female, male or non-binary writers and female or male victims in the original study; women, men and trans people in BBQ; female, male and non-binary people in discrim-eval), read a model's answer as the category it names, and infer no one's gender. Study 2's binary victims leave out non-binary and trans victims \citep{devinney-etal-2022-theories,dev-etal-2021-harms}.

\paragraph{Tests.} Sent one request at a time, as in all llama.cpp runs, the local models repeat an answer exactly (the JEV models within 0.015; Appendix~\ref{app:reliability}), so prompt variants take the place of the original study's repeated chats; Jev does not, and a second pass over all requests measures by how much. In the decision task yes is the favourable outcome (a loan, a transplant, a visa); we send the published texts unchanged, since some are cut off or keep a template tag, with one fixed question (Table~\ref{tab:tests}).

\paragraph{Readouts.} Each answer is read as the mean under the model's probabilities (a probability-level measure, like the original's mean over sampled answers) and as the top answer (the argmax decision an application acts on).

\paragraph{Statistics.} Intervals are 95\% percentile bootstrap intervals \citep{efron-tibshirani-1993-introduction}, as normal approximations are unreliable for few items \citep{bowyer-etal-2025-position}: 4,000 resamples of a measure's units (variants, phrases or items), 2,000 of BBQ templates, whose fillings share a wording, and 2,000 joint resamples of stakes and wordings for gaps pooled over stakes. They describe robustness across these units, not sampling of a model's answers. Tests are two-sided permutation tests with 20,000 permutations: sign flips for paired differences, permuted labels for two-sample comparisons. We declared two families of tests and apply Holm's correction \citep{holm-1979-simple} within each: Study 1's attribution asymmetry and stereotype pull in eight runs, including the JEV models (16 tests), and the decision task's female--male and non-binary--male differences in six runs (12 tests). Other p-values are descriptive. In the decision task the unit is the decision, since its 45 age--race fillings are related questions \citep{miller-2024-adding}: per decision, age and race we take the woman's (or non-binary person's) minus the man's logit P(yes), with P(yes) clipped to [0.005, 0.995] because Jev reports steps of 0.01, and bootstrap and sign-flip the 70 decision means, in the style of the per-template differences \citet{tamkin-etal-2023-discrimination} report beside their mixed model (Appendix~\ref{app:mixed}). Mixed-effects models with random effects for wording and stake (Study 2) and per decision (decision task, simpler than theirs) serve as a robustness check (Appendix~\ref{app:mixed}). Counts over combinations of model, act and stake describe consistency; the combinations share models and wordings and are not independent.

A confidence gate acts only on answers whose confidence, $(k\,p_{\max} - 1)/(k - 1)$ for $k$ options, is at least 0.5, TypeSafe's example floor \citep{typesafe-2026-confidence}. Distances are the mean KL(Jev 1.13 $\|$ model) per request, the direction the JEV cards use, with probabilities floored at 0.005.

\section{Attributing children's writing: a preference for ``girl'' that needs a forced choice}
\label{sec:classification}


Forced to attribute a phrase without a gender cue, clef-flash answers ``girl'' in 85\% of cases and granite-switch in 82\%; pplx answers ``girl'' and ``boy'' about equally often (47\% and 53\%). Jev's most frequent answer is ``non-binary'' (47\%; ``girl'' 40\%, ``boy'' 14\%). Offered \ct, Jev, clef-flash, Kev and d1 abstain on every uncued phrase, pplx on 92\% and granite-switch on 79\% (Figure~\ref{fig:classification}); several models abstain on cued phrases too (Appendix~\ref{app:further}).

GPT-4's attribution asymmetry points the same way in five of the six models, but no asymmetry of theirs is significant after correction. GPT-4's $I_m - I_f$ is $+0.27$; here it is $+0.11$ to $+0.21$ in Jev, pplx, granite-switch and d1, $+0.05$ in clef-flash and $-0.08$ in Kev. After Holm correction over the 16 tests of the Study 1 family, the lowest adjusted p among the six is 0.074 (d1; Jev 0.075); only JEV-9B's asymmetry stays below 0.05 ($p = 0.019$). Measured from each model's own level for uncued phrases (the stereotype pull), no difference survives the correction (Appendix~\ref{app:holm}).

When a BBQ question gives no evidence about which of two people fits a stereotyped role, the models differ most in their abstention rate; when they commit, most name the person the stereotype points to, on gender and in eight other categories (Figures~\ref{fig:benchmarks} and~\ref{fig:bbqcat}).

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Abstention and direction separate.} In the 570 gender questions Jev commits to a person in none, pplx in 8\%, Kev in 13\%, d1 in 22\%, clef-flash in 33\% and granite-switch in 61\%. All five open models mostly name the stereotyped person (0.59 to 0.81); only Kev's interval includes 0.50. BBQ's $s_\mathrm{AMB}$ scales this direction ($2 \times$ share $- 1$) by the error rate and so ranks pplx ($+0.05$) next to Kev ($+0.02$), although pplx's committed answers are more one-sided (0.81 against 0.59).
\item \textbf{Beyond gender the direction holds.} Every model--category cell with at least 10 committed answers leans toward the stereotyped person (41 cells, 0.55 to 1.00), and in 37 the interval excludes 0.50.
\item \textbf{StereoSet barely separates the models:} all prefer the stereotyped sentence, in 64\% to 77\% of items.
\end{itemize}

\section{Decisions about a person}
\label{sec:decision}

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig-decision.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{Changing only the gender moves P(yes) and changes some top answers.} Left: difference in logit P(yes) between the woman (dot) or the non-binary person (diamond) and the man in the same decision, age and race, averaged per decision, with 95\% intervals that resample the 70 decisions; positive favours the woman or the non-binary person. Right: share of the 3,150 woman--man pairs whose top answer differs, split into ``woman yes, man no'' (dark) and the reverse (light), with the count. Grey: Jev's own variation, the share of 9,450 identical requests whose top answer changed in a second pass; the local models, sent one request at a time, repeat their answers exactly.}
\label{fig:decision}

In the decision task of \citet{tamkin-etal-2023-discrimination}, five of the six models give women a significantly higher probability of the favourable answer than men (Figure~\ref{fig:decision}). The difference in log-odds is $+0.050$ (Jev), $+0.058$ (Kev), $+0.080$ (pplx), $+0.082$ (granite-switch) and $+0.097$ (clef-flash), each significant after Holm correction over the 12 tests of this family (adjusted $p < 0.001$); d1's $+0.018$ is not ($p = 0.11$). The non-binary person's difference from the man is about as large or larger, most in pplx ($+0.210$), and significant in the same five models; the mixed-effects estimates agree to the third decimal (Appendix~\ref{app:mixed}). In probability these effects are small: at most 1.5 percentage points in mean P(yes) for women against men and 2.2 for non-binary people (both clef-flash); on the scale of \citet{tamkin-etal-2023-discrimination}, where $+1.0$ raises a P(yes) of 0.5 to 0.73, pplx's $+0.21$ raises it to 0.55. The direction is the one they report for Claude 2.

Taking the top answer, the swap from man to woman changes 34 (granite-switch) to 110 (Jev) of 3,150 decisions, mostly to yes (Jev 102 of 110, clef-flash 77 of 99, Kev 71 of 81, granite-switch 27 of 34); pplx's change about equally often each way (22 and 17), d1's more often to no (25 and 35). Identical requests change Jev's top answer in 135 of 9,450 cases (1.4\%), so a single change of Jev's cannot be told from its variation between calls, though their direction can. Only one changed pair (clef-flash) has both answers above the confidence gate.

\section{From probability to decision}
\label{sec:argmax}

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig-lean.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{The top answer makes a lean firmer.} Ring: mean probability (probability-level measure) of ``girl'' rather than ``boy'' for the eight uncued phrases, of the stereotyped person among the two people in BBQ gender questions without evidence, and of the stereotyped sentence among the two meaningful ones in StereoSet. Dot: share of top answers that choose it (argmax decision). 0.5 is no lean; a dot right of its ring means the top answer makes the lean firmer. BBQ: Jev's top answers name no person and JEV-27B's two.}
\label{fig:lean}
\end{figure*}

Acting on the top answer turns a lean into a firm decision, or hides it (Figure~\ref{fig:lean}). clef-flash puts a mean probability of 0.59 on ``girl'' for uncued writing, but 89\% of its gendered top answers are ``girl''; pplx's lean toward the stereotyped person in BBQ is 0.65, and 81\% of its committed answers name that person; Jev's harassment gap is $+1.69$ points in the mean rating and $+4.5$ in its top answers. The top answer also hides leans: Jev never commits to a person in an ambiguous BBQ gender question, yet the less than 1\% of probability it puts on the two people falls 81\% on the stereotyped one.

A confidence gate, which the makers advise, removes many biased decisions but mostly not their direction. Of clef-flash's 123 ``girl'' answers to uncued phrases 3 pass, but granite-switch still acts on 70 ``girl'' against 18 ``boy''. In ambiguous BBQ gender questions, granite-switch would still act on 180 stereotype-consistent answers against 32 others (0.85, against 0.76 without the gate), clef-flash on 67 against 12 and pplx on 16 against none; d1's gated answers are less one-sided (20 against 10), and Jev and Kev act on none.

\section{What distillation keeps of Jev}
\label{sec:distill}

AutoTrust describes JEV-27B and JEV-9B as open students of Jev 1.13: the JEV-9B card names Jev 1.13 ``the \emph{teacher} whose published output distributions this model was trained on'' and calls JEV ``an independent open-weights \emph{student} built by AutoTrust AI from the Apache-2.0 corpus'' \texttt{SargeDev/\allowbreak jev-distill-corpus-v3}, and ``not affiliated with, endorsed by, or a product of TypeSafe AI'' \citep{autotrust-2026-jev9b}. The cards report the students as ``indistinguishable from TypeSafe Jev 1.13, by KL'' (mean KL 0.028 for JEV-9B and 0.025 for JEV-27B on held-out choice questions labelled by Jev), for ``Jev-labelled rows from the 53 training domains'' \citep{autotrust-2026-jev9b,autotrust-2026-jev27b}. Their moral-trade-off gap and Jev's own changes between calls and versions are in Appendix~\ref{app:further}.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{On our bias questions their KL to Jev 1.13 is 3 to 9 times what the cards report for their own test set:} JEV-27B 0.07 to 0.12, JEV-9B 0.17 to 0.25, while Jev 1.14's two passes are at most 0.003 apart (Table~\ref{tab:distance} in Appendix~\ref{app:further}). JEV-9B is on the whole no closer to Jev than pplx, whose card describes training data built from public datasets \citep{perplexity-2026-pplx}, though closer than its own backbone.
\item \textbf{JEV-9B resembles clef-flash, the other decision model on its backbone, more than its teacher.} On uncued phrases it answers ``girl'' in 94\% of cases (clef-flash 85\%, its teacher 23\%) and never ``non-binary'' (Jev 1.13 66\%); JEV-27B keeps some of the teacher's ``non-binary'' answers (15\%).
\end{itemize}

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The most consistent result is a set of acceptability ratings that disfavour male victims: in every model, act and stake, violence against a man is rated more acceptable when it serves a greater good. The base models show it too (Appendix~\ref{app:controls}), and so do humans \citep{feldmanhall-etal-2016-moral}; by our bias statement it is a bias all the same. In decisions the same direction appears at a small size: five of six models favour women significantly, and a gender swap changes 1\% to 3.5\% of a model's decisions, almost none confidently.

The other biases depend on how the question is put: with an abstention option the models abstain on uncued writing, and in ambiguous BBQ questions they commit in 0\% to 61\% of cases and then mostly follow the stereotype, which BBQ's standard score hides by rewarding abstention.

We found no bias measurement in the makers' cards and announcements, so the deployer must measure the deployed model: a student reported as indistinguishable from its teacher differs from it here, a closed model changes with its version under an unchanged alias (Appendix~\ref{app:further}), and Jev's answers vary between identical calls.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Jev and five open decision models rate violence against men as more acceptable when it serves a greater good; the open models mostly follow stereotypes when they commit; and five of the six favour women slightly in decisions. The top answer turns mild leans into firm decisions; a confidence gate removes many, mostly not their direction. A bias measurement holds for one model, version and configuration, read as the application reads it.

\section*{Limitations}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Scenarios and items.} Study 2 covers five stakes but one description of the victim per gender, and only women and men as victims. The preference for ``girl'' rests on eight phrases without a gender cue that we wrote. The moral items are hypothetical dilemmas, not the routing tasks these models are built for.
\item \textbf{Jev.} We see Jev only through its API, on one date: the results hold for \texttt{jev-1.14.0} and \texttt{jev-1.13.0} as served on 28 October 2026. Its answers vary between identical calls; Jev 1.13, the reference for the distances, ran once.
\item \textbf{Precision and serving.} The two 27B models ran in 8-bit, not in their released 16-bit precision. The open models ran in llama.cpp or vLLM; only Kev was compared with its maker's own server.
\item \textbf{The JEV models.} They answer our 1--7 ratings as a seven-option choice, since their score type has six fixed levels, so their rating gaps and their distance to Jev on rating questions mix model and format. They did not run the decision task.
\item \textbf{Controls.} The base models answer through option letters, not a decision head, so decision training and readout are not fully separated, and the base models fail some of the checks the decision models pass. granite-switch's two interfaces may differ in more than the readout.
\item \textbf{Benchmark samples.} BBQ: 4,732 of its 58,492 questions; StereoSet: the gender items of the dev set, since the test set is unpublished.
\item \textbf{Decision task.} The texts are used as published, including fillings that are cut off or keep a template tag, with one fixed question wording; the demographic categories are those of the dataset, and gender is stated explicitly rather than implied by a name.
\item \textbf{Statistics.} Intervals and tests treat variants, phrases, templates or decisions as units and describe robustness across them, not sampling of a model's answers. Only the two declared families are corrected for multiple comparisons.
\item \textbf{Scope.} English only, no human baseline of our own, and no deployed system.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Ethical considerations}

A guess from base rates can be calibrated and still be no lawful basis for treating one person differently: in \emph{Test-Achats} the Court of Justice of the EU held invalid, with effect from 21 December 2012, Article 5(2) of Directive 2004/113/EC, which let Member States permit sex-based differences in insurance premiums and benefits where sex was a determining factor in the assessment of risk based on relevant and accurate actuarial and statistical data \citep{cjeu-2011-test-achats}, and the EU AI Act requires providers of high-risk systems to examine their data for biases likely to lead to prohibited discrimination \citep[Article 10]{eu-2024-ai-act}. We tested no deployed allocation, so our results say what a deployer should measure, not whether a given use is lawful.

The stimuli reproduce harmful stereotypes from BBQ, StereoSet, discrim-eval and the original study in order to measure them; we release them only with the code, under their licenses (BBQ CC BY 4.0, StereoSet CC BY-SA 4.0, discrim-eval CC BY 4.0). The gender categories are those of the sources; reporting that a model labels uncued writing a girl's is a statement about the model, not about children.

As \citet{biderman-etal-2024-lessons} advise, we release the code, the stimuli and the per-request answers of every open model, and also all numbers derived from Jev's answers (\texttt{results/summary.md}, \texttt{figures.json}, \texttt{mixed.json}). We do not release Jev's raw answers: TypeSafe's terms forbid using its output ``to perform model distillation, train a model to imitate the output of the Services, or develop \ldots{} a similar or competing product or service'' \citep[Section 2.3(b)]{typesafe-2026-mca}. We used Jev's answers for this evaluation only. Anyone with a TypeSafe API key can rerun the requests with \texttt{run.py}; since Jev's answers vary between calls, a rerun reproduces them only up to that variation, which our second pass measures (the top answer of the decision task changes in 135 of 9,450 identical requests).

\paragraph{Compute.} Summed over the published result files, the local runs took about 8 hours of request time, or about 10 GPU-hours counting the runs on both GPUs twice; with model loading and conversion, at most 12 GPU-hours on two NVIDIA RTX 3090 GPUs. All requests to TypeSafe's API cost about US\$1, at \$0.042 per million input tokens.

\paragraph{Use of AI assistants.} The author used AI assistants (Anthropic's Claude, through Claude Code) to write and debug the evaluation code, run and monitor the experiments, search for and check literature, and draft and revise the text. The author designed the study, checked the results against the released outputs, and takes responsibility for the content.

\section{Serving details}
\label{app:serving}

\texttt{results/servers-z390.json} lists, for every local run, the weights (GGUF file, SHA-256 and source repository at a pinned revision, or Hugging Face revision and decision-head hashes), the GPUs and the full server arguments.

\paragraph{API-compatible models.} One \texttt{llama-server} per model (llama.cpp build 11870, commit 74bca3950, in Docker) with a context of 8,192 tokens and batches of 2,048 tokens; the 27B models on both GPUs, the others on one. pplx-decider's Q8\_0 file was converted from \texttt{perplexity-ai/pplx-decider-v1.1-27b} at revision 14c9d901 with llama.cpp b11870 (\texttt{--convert --outtype q8\_0}); its header carries the decision type (\texttt{pplx-decider}), the calibration temperature and the request template, as the published 4-bit conversion does.

\paragraph{A KV-cache trap in llama-server.} For a decision model that also generates text, \texttt{llama-server} reuses the cached attention state of the previous prompt and computes only the rest; decision requests ignore \texttt{--no-cache-prompt}. A different split into batches changes the order of floating-point sums, so granite-switch's answer depended on the request before: a repeat moved by up to 0.017, four requests at once by 0.114. Emptying the slot before every request made it repeat exactly; all granite-switch decision results use that.

\paragraph{Distilled models.} vLLM 0.32.0 with each model's LoRA adapter (rank 32), at most 16 sequences at a time. JEV-27B's bf16 weights (54\,GB) do not fit in two 24\,GB GPUs, so vLLM quantized them to fp8 on load (weight-only) and split the model over both GPUs. We read the decision head as the reference client on the model cards does, which reproduces the cards' two worked examples to within 0.01.

\paragraph{Analysis.} \texttt{analyze.py} uses only Python 3.12's standard library; \texttt{mixed.py} uses statsmodels 0.15.0 and pandas 3.0.0.

\paragraph{Jev.} TypeSafe's \texttt{/v1/systemone} endpoint, eight requests at a time; \texttt{run.py} requires every response to name the versioned model asked for. Provenance is in \texttt{results/api-typesafe.json}.

\section{Further results}
\label{app:further}

\paragraph{Moral trade-offs.} Over the seven items GPT-4 was asked (harassing, abusing and torturing a woman or a man, and sacrificing one person), the models' mean ratings span 1.2 (Kev) to 3.3 points (Jev), GPT-4's 3.7. Relative to that span the harassment gap is 0.73 to 0.87 in pplx, clef-flash, granite-switch and Kev, 0.51 in Jev and 0.31 in d1 (GPT-4 0.63); the abuse gap is at most 0.55 (pplx; GPT-4 0.86). GPT-4's 4.65-point vignette contrast is 0.28 to 0.88 here, and 0.00 in d1; Qwen3.8-27B gives 0.42 through option letters and 0.40 with generated answers, so the shrinkage is not an artefact of reading probabilities. Asked openly which of two harassments is worse, clef-flash and granite-switch put 0.95 and 0.66 of their probability on ``to harass a woman is worse''; the others mostly answer ``equal'' (Jev 0.93, Kev 0.88, pplx 0.71, d1 0.63), and GPT-4's open ranking showed no gender difference. Read from the top answers, the harassment gap under the apocalypse is 5.35 points for pplx and 4.5 for Jev.

\paragraph{Children's writing.} With the abstention option, the models also abstain on cued phrases: for feminine-coded phrases Kev, d1 and Jev answer \ct{} in 75\%, 69\% and 65\% of cases, pplx in 20\% and granite-switch in 4\%. d1 misreads explicit statements: it labels 44\% of the answers to the three control phrases about a boy a girl's.

\paragraph{BBQ and StereoSet.} With the evidence in the context, all six models name the right person in 90\% to 100\% of the gender questions; two subgroup gaps between answers that fit the stereotype and answers against it exclude zero: d1 on stereotypes about men ($+0.14$, interval 0.06 to 0.24) and Kev on stereotypes about trans people ($+0.14$, 0.02 to 0.29). In StereoSet, female and male targets differ by at most 5.6 points in any model ($p > 0.17$).

\paragraph{Distillation.} Where Jev 1.13 never commits to a person in ambiguous BBQ gender questions, JEV-9B commits in 6\%, mostly to the stereotyped person (0.79). Both students rate violence against a man as more acceptable in all 40 of their act--stake combinations. Whether their gap is smaller than the teacher's depends on the scale: in the mean rating, which the students answer as a seven-option choice, their harassment effect is smaller than Jev 1.14's (mixed model: $+1.05$ and $+0.96$ against $+1.65$); in logit P(yes), a format they share with Jev, JEV-27B's is as large ($+1.68$ against $+1.56$) and JEV-9B's smaller ($+0.95$). Jev itself moves: over all 19,405 requests its two passes are 0.001 apart in mean KL and versions 1.13 and 1.14 0.011; with 1.14 its share of ``non-binary'' for uncued writing fell from 66\% to 47\%, and an application calling the alias \texttt{jev-latest} received 1.14 without a change of code \citep{typesafe-2026-models}.

\section{Mixed-effects models}
\label{app:mixed}

\paragraph{Specification.} Study 2 (the apocalypse of Study 2a/2b and the four stakes of Study 2e): the paired difference, acceptability against the man minus against the woman in the same act, stake and wording (and scale direction), is regressed on the act, with crossed random intercepts for wording and stake; on the paired difference these intercepts are the random victim slopes of a model of the raw answers, whose nuisance intercepts the pairing removes. It is fitted once for the mean rating on the 1--7 scale and once for logit P(yes). Decision task: logit P(yes) on gender, race and age in decades, with a random intercept and random gender slopes per decision; the reference person is a 60-year-old white man, as in \citet{tamkin-etal-2023-discrimination}, who also give each decision random slopes for race and age, z-score age, and report per-template differences with the standard error of the mean, where we bootstrap and sign-flip decision means. All models are fitted by REML with Powell's method in statsmodels 0.15.0 (\texttt{mixed.py}); intervals are 95\% Wald intervals. Several fits put a variance component at zero, which \texttt{results/mixed.md} notes per fit.
