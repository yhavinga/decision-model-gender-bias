\begin{abstract}
Decision models are off-the-shelf language models that return option probabilities for typed questions in one forward pass; applications act on the top answer, for example to classify or route. They are trained for the decision format, not for fairness or for one application, and we found no published bias measurement. We test TypeSafe's closed Jev and five open decision models on gender-swapped yes/no decisions about people \citep{tamkin-etal-2023-discrimination}, BBQ, StereoSet and the gender-bias studies of \citet{fulgu-capraro-2024-surprising}. Five of the six give women significantly more favourable answers than men (+0.05 to +0.10 in log-odds), and a gender swap changes 1--3.5\% of top answers (Jev's partly through its call-to-call variation). The makers' recommended confidence gate does not remove biased commitments: in ambiguous BBQ gender questions it raises the stereotyped share of the answers it keeps (granite-switch 0.76 to 0.85, pplx 0.81 to 1.00). Probes show gender preferences in the same models: all six rate violence for a greater good as more acceptable against a man than against a woman, and two default to ``girl'' when forced to guess a child's gender without a cue. Decision-level and probability-level measures differ, so audits should read a model as its application does.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Decision models are built to act. In September 2026 TypeSafe AI released Jev, ``a new class of frontier models built to make fast, structured decisions that software can use directly'' \citep{typesafe-2026-jev}: it answers typed questions (pick one of these options, score this on a scale, yes or no) with calibrated option probabilities from one forward pass, to ``classify, route, score, extract, or branch'' \citep{typesafe-2026-jev}, and it reports a confidence on which applications set thresholds \citep{typesafe-2026-confidence}. Open models from Perplexity, Cloudflare, IBM, Liquid AI and Jared Palmer answer the same typed questions and can be served through llama.cpp's \texttt{/v1/systemone} endpoint \citep{perplexity-2026-pplx,cloudflare-2026-clef-flash,ibm-2026-granite-switch,liquidai-2026-d1,palmer-2026-kev}. We use ``decision model'' for this model class: a component, not an automated decision-making (ADM) system, which also comprises the application that acts on its answers; but unless the application adds review, the model's answer is the decision.

Gender biases of chat language models are documented (Section~\ref{sec:related}). What is new is the deployment context. Decision models are offered off the shelf: Jev ``is not fine-tuned or LoRA-adapted with customer data'', and ``the same weights serve every account'' \citep{typesafe-2026-models}. They are trained for the decision format, not for fairness or for any one application, and their calibrated probabilities invite thresholds. Two makers warn against consequential use: AutoTrust writes ``Not for high-stakes decisions'' \citep{autotrust-2026-jev9b}, and Kev's card says ``Do not use it for consequential automated decisions about people without human review. Biases of the base model and of the training data \ldots{} are not measured'' \citep{palmer-2026-kev}. We found no bias measurement in the cards and announcements we checked.

We test Jev and five open decision models where their output becomes a decision, and probe the gender preferences behind those decisions. Our contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Decision-level evidence: a gender-swapped decision task \citep{tamkin-etal-2023-discrimination}, the makers' confidence gate, and a comparison of decision-level (argmax) with probability-level measures.
\item Probes of the underlying preferences: the paradigms of \citet{fulgu-capraro-2024-surprising}, extended with prompt variants, five stakes and an abstention option; BBQ in nine categories; and StereoSet.
\item Controls for base model, interface, precision, serving path and version, and a comparison of two open models described as students of Jev with Jev.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Gender asymmetries in moral judgements.} \citet{fulgu-capraro-2024-surprising} reported seven studies on GPT models; we follow the numbering of this arXiv version (journal version: \citealp{fulgu-capraro-2024-chbr}). Repeating both paradigms with ten models, \citet{bolzoni-capraro-2026-gender} found a ``male-disadvantaging asymmetry'' in several models' moral judgements and mixed directions in the attribution task. Language models also frame male actors more punitively for the same misconduct \citep{si-etal-2026-harsher}, judge female characters more mildly in otherwise identical stories \citep{bajaj-etal-2024-evaluating}, prefer to save women in trolley-style dilemmas more strongly than humans do \citep{takemoto-2024-moral,jin-etal-2025-language}, and favour female candidates in hiring tasks \citep{rozado-2026-gender,an-etal-2025-measuring}. None of these studies tests decision models.

\paragraph{Probes and applications.} \citet{blodgett-etal-2021-stereotyping} question what benchmarks such as BBQ \citep{parrish-etal-2022-bbq} and StereoSet \citep{nadeem-etal-2021-stereoset} measure. Intrinsic bias metrics do not reliably correlate with bias in downstream applications \citep{goldfarb-tarrant-etal-2021-intrinsic,cao-etal-2022-intrinsic}, nor standard bias metrics with bias in more realistic use \citep{lum-etal-2025-bias}; we therefore lead with decisions and use the probes to show the preferences behind them. BBQ results change with the prompt \citep{hida-etal-2025-social,mizrahi-etal-2024-state}; its ambiguous-context score mixes how often a model commits to a person with the direction of those answers \citep{manduru-domeniconi-2026-beyond}; and forcing a choice changes what models answer \citep{rottger-etal-2024-political}.

\paragraph{Decisions and amplification.} \citet{tamkin-etal-2023-discrimination} measure discrimination in yes/no decisions about people with the normalized probability of ``yes'', the readout decision models provide natively, and find positive discrimination toward women and non-binary people in Claude 2; \citet{zollo-etal-2025-towards} relate such tests to the doctrines of disparate treatment and disparate impact. Bias amplification denotes predictions that show a larger disparity than the training data or ground truth \citep{zhao-etal-2017-men,leino-etal-2019-feature}, measured directionally by \citet{wang-russakovsky-2021-directional}. Within one model, taking the argmax of a consistent probability-level preference produces a larger disparity in decisions than in probabilities; we report this decision-level amplification next to the probability-level measure. First-token probabilities and generated answers often disagree \citep{wang-etal-2024-answer-c}, option order matters \citep{zheng-etal-2024-robust}, and 4-bit quantization flips individual bias answers \citep{hua-etal-2026-quantized}.

\section{Bias statement}
\label{sec:bias}

Following \citet{blodgett-etal-2020-language}, we state which system behaviours we count as bias, why they are harmful, and to whom, distinguishing allocational from representational harms \citep{gallegos-etal-2024-bias}.

\paragraph{What we count as bias.} (i) A \emph{counterfactual (gender-swapped) difference} \citep{garg-etal-2019-counterfactual}: an output that changes when only the stated gender of a person changes (the decision task, Study 2, the vignette of Study 2d). (ii) A \emph{stereotype-consistent answer without evidence}: in ambiguous BBQ questions, committing to the person a stereotype points to. (iii) A \emph{default preference} for one gender when no gender cue is present (uncued phrases in Study 1), whether for ``girl'', ``boy'' or ``non-binary''. (iv) More probability on a stereotyped than on an anti-stereotyped completion (StereoSet). Following evidence in the input is accuracy, and choosing ``unknown'' or ``can't tell'' when information is missing is correct.

\paragraph{Harms, and on whom they fall.} In the decision task, a lower probability of the favourable answer for one gender would be disparate treatment if an application acted on it: an allocational harm, which falls on men in five of our six models (Section~\ref{sec:decision}). The other tests measure answers that are not allocations, so we treat them as representational harms: ratings that disfavour male victims (all six models); defaults that label boys' writing a girl's (clef-flash, granite-switch) or say ``non-binary'' (Jev); and stereotype-consistent answers in BBQ about women, men and trans people (trans people most often in pplx, clef-flash and d1; Appendix~\ref{app:bbqx}) and the groups of eight more categories.

\paragraph{Ratings that disfavour male victims.} We hold that the acceptability of harming a person should not depend on the person's gender, so ratings that judge violence against a man as more acceptable are a bias under (i). This is a normative position, not an empirical finding. Humans show a similar asymmetry, called moral chivalry \citep{feldmanhall-etal-2016-moral}, and a subjectively positive but paternalistic orientation toward women is what \citet{glick-fiske-1996-ambivalent} call benevolent sexism; a model can match human judgement and still be biased by our criterion. The ratings are hypothetical judgements, so their harm is representational: men's suffering weighs less, and women appear in need of protection.

\paragraph{Gender terms.} We use each source's categories (female, male or non-binary writers and female or male victims in the original study; women, men and trans people in BBQ; female, male and non-binary people in discrim-eval), read a model's answer as the category it names, and infer no one's gender. Study 2's binary victims leave out non-binary and trans victims \citep{devinney-etal-2022-theories,dev-etal-2021-harms}.

\paragraph{Tests.} Table~\ref{tab:tests} lists the tests and Appendix~\ref{app:examples} gives one request per study as sent. All llama.cpp runs sent one request at a time; under that condition the local models repeat an answer exactly (the JEV models within 0.015 in a serial check; Appendix~\ref{app:reliability}). Prompt variants (wordings, option orders, both scale directions) therefore take the place of the original study's repeated chats. Jev does not repeat exactly; a second pass over all requests measures by how much. The decision-task texts are sent as published, since some are cut off or keep a template tag, with one fixed question; yes is the favourable outcome (a loan, a transplant, a visa).

\paragraph{Measures.} A \emph{probability-level} measure averages the model's probabilities, as the original's mean over sampled answers estimates a mean answer; a \emph{decision-level} measure counts top (argmax) answers, the ones an application acting on the output uses. In BBQ, a committed answer names one of the two people rather than ``unknown''. The confidence gate keeps only answers whose confidence, $(k\,p_{\max} - 1)/(k - 1)$ for $k$ options, is at least 0.5, TypeSafe's example floor \citep{typesafe-2026-confidence}.

\paragraph{Statistics.} Intervals are 95\% percentile bootstrap intervals \citep{efron-tibshirani-1993-introduction}, as normal approximations are unreliable for few items \citep{bowyer-etal-2025-position}: 4,000 resamples of a measure's units (variants, phrases or items), 2,000 of BBQ templates, whose fillings share a wording, and 2,000 joint resamples of stakes and wordings for gaps pooled over stakes. They describe robustness across these units, not sampling of a model's answers, and are optimistic where few units are resampled (eight uncued phrases, five stakes) or both scale directions are pooled. Tests are two-sided permutation tests with 20,000 permutations. Two families of tests, defined in the analysis code and not pre-registered, are corrected with Holm's method \citep{holm-1979-simple}: in Study 1, the attribution asymmetry and the difference between how far feminine phrases move the answer toward ``girl'' and masculine phrases toward ``boy'' (16 tests over eight runs, the JEV models included); and the decision task's woman--man and non-binary--man differences (12 tests over six runs). Other p-values are descriptive. In the decision task the unit is the decision, since its 45 age--race fillings are related questions \citep{miller-2024-adding}: per decision, age and race we take the woman's (or non-binary person's) minus the man's logit P(yes), with P(yes) clipped to [0.005, 0.995] because Jev reports steps of 0.01, and bootstrap and sign-flip the 70 decision means, in the style of the per-template differences of \citet{tamkin-etal-2023-discrimination}. Mixed-effects models serve as a robustness check (Appendix~\ref{app:mixed}); several fit a variance component at zero, where Wald intervals are unreliable.

\paragraph{Gender-swapped decisions.} Five of the six models give women a significantly higher probability of the favourable answer than men (Figure~\ref{fig:decision}): $+0.050$ (Jev), $+0.058$ (Kev), $+0.080$ (pplx), $+0.082$ (granite-switch) and $+0.097$ (clef-flash) in log-odds, each with a Holm-adjusted $p < 0.001$; d1's $+0.018$ is not significant ($p = 0.11$). The non-binary person's difference from the man is about as large or larger, most in pplx ($+0.210$), and significant in the same five models; the mixed-effects estimates agree to the third decimal. The direction is the one \citet{tamkin-etal-2023-discrimination} report for Claude 2. The effects are small: at most 1.5 percentage points in mean P(yes) for women and 2.2 for non-binary people (both clef-flash); on Tamkin et al.'s scale, where $+1.0$ raises a P(yes) of 0.5 to 0.73, pplx's $+0.21$ raises it to 0.55. Over the dataset's ages from 20 to 100, age moves the answer more in two models: $-0.040$ (granite-switch) and $-0.022$ (clef-flash) per decade, about $-0.32$ and $-0.18$ over the 80 years; the race effects (each group against white) range from $-0.012$ to $+0.196$, all but one positive (Appendix~\ref{app:mixed}).

At the decision level, swapping the man for a woman changes 34 (granite-switch) to 110 (Jev) of 3,150 top answers, mostly to yes (Jev 102 of 110, clef-flash 77 of 99, Kev 71 of 81, granite-switch 27 of 34); pplx's changes go both ways (22 and 17), d1's more often to no (25 and 35). Jev's changes include its variation between calls: a second pass changes its top answer in 135 of 9,450 identical requests. Only one changed pair (clef-flash) has both answers above the confidence gate.

\paragraph{From probability to decision.} Taking the argmax of a consistent preference yields a larger disparity in decisions than in probabilities (Figure~\ref{fig:lean}), the decision-level counterpart of bias amplification \citep{zhao-etal-2017-men,leino-etal-2019-feature}; any threshold does this to a preference that points the same way across items. clef-flash puts a mean probability of 0.59 on ``girl'' for uncued phrases, but 89\% of its gendered top answers are ``girl''; pplx's mean probability on the stereotyped person in ambiguous BBQ gender questions is 0.65, and 81\% of its committed answers name that person; Jev's harassment gap under the apocalypse is $+1.69$ points in the mean rating and $+4.5$ in its top answers.

\paragraph{The confidence gate.} The makers advise acting only on confident answers \citep{typesafe-2026-confidence}. In ambiguous BBQ gender questions the gate removes many committed answers, but the stereotyped ones survive more often, so their share among the answers it keeps rises: granite-switch from 0.76 to 0.85 (212 of 347 answers kept), clef-flash from 0.76 to 0.85 (79 of 190) and pplx from 0.81 to 1.00 (16 of 43). d1 is the exception (0.72 to 0.67, 30 of 124 kept); none of Kev's committed answers passes, and Jev commits to none. For uncued phrases the gate removes almost all of clef-flash's ``girl'' answers (3 of 123 pass), but granite-switch still acts on 70 ``girl'' against 18 ``boy''.

\section{The preferences behind the decisions}
\label{sec:probes}

\paragraph{Moral trade-offs.} All six models rate violence for a greater good as more acceptable against a man than against a woman (Figure~\ref{fig:moral}). For harassment, abuse and torture, and for insults, which are not violence, the mean-rating gap is positive in every model and stake; over all 120 combinations of model, act and stake the smallest is d1's $+0.03$ (torture, kidnapped child), and read from the top answers 85 show a gap and none reverses it. The mixed-effects estimates are positive for every model and act, and 22 of their 24 intervals exclude zero (granite-switch's for abuse and d1's for torture do not). Under the original's apocalypse, pplx's harassment gap ($+2.76$ points) exceeds GPT-4's ($+2.34$); Jev's is $+1.69$. Without a stake the gaps for harassment, abuse and torture are small ($-0.02$ to $+0.22$), while insulting a woman stays worse than insulting a man in four models. The original's vignette result does not replicate: GPT-4's contrast of 4.65 points, from chat answers reported in 2024, is 0.00 to 0.88 here, and 0.40 for Qwen3.8-27B's generated answers (Appendix~\ref{app:moral}).

\paragraph{Children's writing without a gender cue.} Forced to choose, two open models default to ``girl'' (Figure~\ref{fig:classification}): clef-flash in 85\% and granite-switch in 82\% of their answers; pplx answers ``girl'' and ``boy'' about equally often (47\% and 53\%). Jev's default is ``non-binary'' (47\%; ``girl'' 40\%, ``boy'' 14\%), which depends on the wording: 60\% when asked to imagine a writer, 33\% when asked who most likely wrote the phrase (Appendix~\ref{app:writing}). Offered \ct, Jev, clef-flash, Kev and d1 choose it for every uncued phrase, pplx for 92\% and granite-switch for 79\%. The eight uncued phrases are ours, written to carry no gender cue but not normed with human raters (Appendix~\ref{app:data}). GPT-4's attribution asymmetry ($I_m - I_f = +0.27$) points the same way in five of the six models ($+0.05$ to $+0.21$; Kev $-0.08$), with similar values for both kinds of wording, but none is significant after Holm correction (lowest adjusted $p$ 0.074, d1); of the eight runs in the family, only JEV-9B's is ($p = 0.019$).

\paragraph{Answers without evidence: BBQ and StereoSet.} In ambiguous BBQ questions the models differ most in how often they commit to a person rather than choose ``unknown''; when they commit, most name the person the stereotype points to (Figures~\ref{fig:benchmarks} and~\ref{fig:bbqcat}). In the 570 gender questions Jev commits in none, pplx in 8\%, Kev in 13\%, d1 in 22\%, clef-flash in 33\% and granite-switch in 61\%; the five open models' committed answers name the stereotyped person in 0.59 (Kev, whose interval includes 0.50) to 0.81 (pplx) of cases. BBQ's $s_\mathrm{AMB}$ scales this direction, $2 \times \mathrm{share} - 1$, by the error rate, so it conflates commitment and direction: pplx ($+0.05$) and Kev ($+0.02$) score alike although their committed answers differ (0.81 against 0.59). In the eight other categories, every model--category cell with at least 10 committed answers leans toward the stereotyped person (41 cells, 0.55 to 1.00), and in 37 the interval excludes 0.50. In StereoSet all six prefer the stereotyped sentence, in 64\% to 77\% of items.

\section{Controls}
\label{sec:controls}

The moral-trade-off gap is already present in the base models, read through option letters; the decision models differ from their bases most in the default for ``girl'' (clef-flash 85\%, its base 51\%; Appendix~\ref{app:controls}). granite-switch commits to a person in 61\% of the ambiguous BBQ gender questions through its decision interface and in 9\% through its text interface, consistent with the separate decision adapter its card describes \citep{ibm-2026-granite-switch}. 4-bit files move single answers more than aggregates, and Kev through its maker's own server gives the same top answer as through llama.cpp in 151 of 151 sampled requests (Appendix~\ref{app:reliability}). Jev changes between calls (a second pass is 0.001 from the first in mean KL) and between versions: from 1.13 to 1.14 its share of ``non-binary'' for uncued writing fell from 66\% to 47\%, and an application calling the alias \texttt{jev-latest} received 1.14 without a change of code \citep{typesafe-2026-models}. AutoTrust's students are 3 to 9 times further from Jev 1.13 on our questions than their cards report for their own test set, which our questions lie outside (Appendix~\ref{app:jev}).

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The decision-level evidence is small but consistent: five of six models favour women in decisions about a person, and a gender swap changes 1\% to 3.5\% of a model's decisions, almost none confidently. Over the dataset's age range, age moves decisions more than gender in two models, but a difference by gender is a difference in treatment whatever its size. The probes show preferences that can produce such differences: ratings that disfavour male victims in every model, as base models and humans \citep{feldmanhall-etal-2016-moral} also do, defaults without a gender cue, and stereotype-consistent answers without evidence. Their size depends on how the question is put and on whether probabilities or top answers are read. Thresholding amplifies a consistent preference, and the makers' gate can concentrate stereotype-consistent answers rather than remove them. Since intrinsic measures need not predict application bias \citep{goldfarb-tarrant-etal-2021-intrinsic,cao-etal-2022-intrinsic}, an audit of a decision model should read it as its application does: at the application's threshold, on the deployed model and version, with several wordings and option orders.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Off-the-shelf decision models make the model's answer the decision. In Jev and five open decision models, gender changes a small share of decisions, mostly in favour of women; the models carry gender preferences in moral ratings, defaults without a cue and stereotype-consistent answers; and the makers' confidence gate does not remove the biased commitments it lets through.

\section*{Limitations}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Scenarios and items.} Study 2 covers five stakes but one description of the victim per gender, and only women and men as victims. The defaults without a gender cue rest on eight phrases we wrote and did not norm with human raters. The moral items are hypothetical dilemmas, not the routing tasks these models are built for.
\item \textbf{Jev.} We see Jev only through its API, on one date, at the API's resolution of 0.01: the results hold for \texttt{jev-1.14.0} and \texttt{jev-1.13.0} as served on 28 October 2026. Its answers vary between identical calls; Jev 1.13 ran once.
\item \textbf{Precision and serving.} The two 27B models ran in 8-bit, not in their released 16-bit precision. The open models ran in llama.cpp or vLLM; only Kev was compared with its maker's own server.
\item \textbf{The JEV models.} They answer our 1--7 ratings as a seven-option choice, since their score type has six fixed levels, so their rating gaps mix model and format. They did not run the decision task.
\item \textbf{Controls.} The base models answer through option letters, not a decision head, so decision training and readout are not fully separated, and the base models fail some of the checks the decision models pass.
\item \textbf{Benchmark samples.} BBQ: 4,732 of its 58,492 questions; StereoSet: the gender items of the dev set, since the test set is unpublished.
\item \textbf{Decision task.} One fixed question wording; the explicit set only, not the implicit set that conveys gender through names; texts as published, including fillings that are cut off or keep a template tag; and clipping P(yes) to [0.005, 0.995] compresses differences for models near the ceiling (granite-switch's mean P(yes) is 0.90).
\item \textbf{Statistics.} Intervals and tests treat variants, phrases, templates or decisions as units and describe robustness across them, not sampling of a model's answers; with eight phrases or five stakes, or pooled scale directions, they are optimistic. Wald intervals of mixed-model fits with a variance component at zero are unreliable. The two families were defined in the analysis code, not pre-registered; other p-values are uncorrected.
\item \textbf{Scope.} English only, no human baseline of our own, and no deployed system.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Ethical considerations}

A guess from base rates can be calibrated and still be no lawful basis for treating one person differently: in \emph{Test-Achats} the Court of Justice of the EU held invalid, with effect from 21 December 2012, Article 5(2) of Directive 2004/113/EC, which let Member States permit sex-based differences in insurance premiums and benefits where sex was a determining factor in the assessment of risk based on relevant and accurate actuarial and statistical data \citep{cjeu-2011-test-achats}. The same directive allows positive action, ``specific measures to prevent or compensate for disadvantages linked to sex'' \citep[Article 6]{eu-2004-directive-113}; such measures are adopted deliberately, and no maker describes the gender differences we measure as one. The EU AI Act requires providers of high-risk systems to examine their data for biases likely to lead to prohibited discrimination \citep[Article 10]{eu-2024-ai-act}. We tested no deployed allocation, so our results say what a deployer should measure, not whether a given use is lawful.

The stimuli reproduce harmful stereotypes from BBQ, StereoSet, discrim-eval and the original study in order to measure them; we release them only with the code, under their licenses (BBQ CC BY 4.0, StereoSet CC BY-SA 4.0, discrim-eval CC BY 4.0). The gender categories are those of the sources; reporting that a model labels uncued writing a girl's is a statement about the model, not about children.

As \citet{biderman-etal-2024-lessons} advise, we release the code, the stimuli and the per-request answers of every open model, and also all numbers derived from Jev's answers (\texttt{results/summary.md}, \texttt{figures.json}, \texttt{mixed.json}). We do not release Jev's raw answers: TypeSafe's terms forbid using its output ``to perform model distillation, train a model to imitate the output of the Services, or develop \ldots{} a similar or competing product or service'' \citep[Section 2.3(b)]{typesafe-2026-mca}. We used Jev's answers for this evaluation only and trained nothing on them. We evaluate AutoTrust's JEV models, whatever their stated provenance, because they are public and presented as indistinguishable from Jev, so their behaviour is of public interest. Anyone with a TypeSafe API key can rerun the requests with \texttt{run.py}; since Jev's answers vary between calls, a rerun reproduces them only up to that variation, which our second pass measures (the top answer of the decision task changes in 135 of 9,450 identical requests).

\paragraph{Compute.} Summed over the published result files, the local runs took about 8 hours of request time, or about 10 GPU-hours counting the runs on both GPUs twice; with model loading and conversion, at most 12 GPU-hours on two NVIDIA RTX 3090 GPUs. All requests to TypeSafe's API cost about US\$1, at \$0.042 per million input tokens.

\paragraph{Use of AI assistants.} The author used AI assistants (Anthropic's Claude, through Claude Code) to write and debug the evaluation code, run and monitor the experiments, search for and check literature, and draft and revise the text. The author designed the study, checked the results against the released outputs, and takes responsibility for the content.

\section{Moral trade-offs: further results}
\label{app:moral}

Over the seven items GPT-4 was asked (harassing, abusing and torturing a woman or a man, and sacrificing one person), the models' mean ratings span 1.2 (Kev) to 3.3 points (Jev), GPT-4's 3.7. Read through option letters, the base models change many top answers when only the option order changes (22\% to 63\% of Study 1 cases) and fail explicit controls, so differences from a decision model are indicative, not causal (Table~\ref{tab:controls}). Within that limit, the moral-trade-off gap is already present in the base models; over five stakes the decision models' harassment gaps are larger than their bases' (pplx $+1.98$ against $+1.49$, clef-flash $+1.25$ against $+0.90$, Kev $+1.09$ against $+0.59$), with overlapping intervals. The default for ``girl'' differs more: clef-flash 85\% against 51\% for its base, Kev 57\% against 74\%, pplx 47\% against 42\%. granite-switch's two interfaces differ most: its decision interface commits in 61\% of the ambiguous BBQ gender questions, its text interface in 9\%, and its harassment gap falls from $+1.51$ to $+0.69$; its card describes a separate decision adapter that control tokens in the chat template activate \citep{ibm-2026-granite-switch}, so the two interfaces need not run the same computation. clef-flash's answers do not change with option order at all (differences of exactly 0), which suggests its decision head scores the options independently of their position; we did not verify this. \paragraph{Serving path.} We sent 151 requests spread over all ten studies (every 128th) to Kev through its maker's own server (\texttt{github.com/jaredpalmer/kev} at commit 2074cc4, \texttt{kev.serve}, PyTorch in bf16, weights \texttt{jaredpalmer/kev-4b} at revision 6cfce5c2, from which the GGUF file was converted). All 151 top answers agree with our llama.cpp answers; probabilities differ by at most 0.0125 and on average by 0.0025, and the mean KL divergence is below 0.0001 (\texttt{results/checks/kev-reference.txt}).

\paragraph{Quantization.} Our first runs of the five open decision models and the base models used 4-bit files (Q4\_K\_S or Q4\_K\_M). Table~\ref{tab:quant} compares them with the main runs over the 9,955 requests of the nine studies other than the decision task. For the decision models the mean KL between precisions is 0.005 to 0.017, far below any open model's distance to Jev 1.13 (Table~\ref{tab:distance}); their apocalypse gaps move by at most 0.37 points (pplx, abuse), their BBQ commitment rates by at most 6 points and StereoSet ss by at most 1.1 points. Shares that rest on few answers move more: pplx's stereotype-consistent share goes from 0.81 to 0.92 (43 and 37 committed answers), Kev's share of uncued phrases called a girl's from 57\% to 44\%. Read through option letters, the base models move more (harassment gaps by up to 0.40 points). granite-switch's text interface moves most (KL 0.142, against 0.017 for its decision interface); rerunning 204 of its text-interface requests without the prompt cache and with an emptied slot gives identical answers (\texttt{results/checks}), so this is an effect of quantization on the text path, not of the cache. The generated answers of Qwen3.8-27B differ by 0.115, which includes the variation of sampling five answers per request. \citet{hua-etal-2026-quantized} found that 4-bit quantization flips individual bias answers in chat models.

\section{Mixed-effects models and multiple comparisons}
\label{app:mixed}

\paragraph{Specification.} Study 2 (the apocalypse of Study 2a/2b and the four stakes of Study 2e): the paired difference, acceptability against the man minus against the woman in the same act, stake and wording (and scale direction), is regressed on the act, with crossed random intercepts for wording and stake; on the paired difference these intercepts are the random victim slopes of a model of the raw answers, whose nuisance intercepts the pairing removes. Decision task: logit P(yes) on gender, race and age in decades, with a random intercept and random gender slopes per decision; the reference person is a 60-year-old white man. \citet{tamkin-etal-2023-discrimination} also give each decision random slopes for race and age, z-score age, and report per-template differences with the standard error of the mean, where we bootstrap and sign-flip decision means. All models are fitted by REML with Powell's method in statsmodels 0.15.0 (\texttt{mixed.py}); intervals are 95\% Wald intervals. Several fits put a variance component at zero, which \texttt{results/mixed.md} notes per fit; their intervals are unreliable.
